Understanding Anger: Unease, Insecurity, and the Stories We Tell Ourselves

A few years ago, during a particularly tense period at work, I watched two senior engineers argue over a design decision.

What struck me was not the argument itself but what happened after: both walked away convinced the other was irrational. Neither had actually said what they meant. One was afraid his work would be undone without credit. The other was exhausted and felt unseen. Neither said that. What came out instead was anger.

That moment sent me down a long inquiry into human emotions, specifically into what anger actually is and what it is not.

Anger as an Expression of Uneasiness

When people don’t know how to express what is happening inside them, it often comes out as anger. Unless someone is consciously using anger as a tool to make a point, an angry outburst usually means the person is struggling to say something important in a calmer way. Anger, then, becomes an expression of uneasiness. The body and mind are not at ease, and that discomfort demands an outlet.

The signal often appears in the body before the mind catches up: a tightening in the chest, a shorter breath, words that come out harder than intended. These are not character flaws. They are the body trying to communicate what the mind has not yet found language for.

Seen this way, an angry person is not just “being difficult”; they are trying, intentionally or not, to communicate something they cannot easily put into words. If anger is really a sign of unease, then the most sensible response is to help the person feel at ease: physically, mentally, and emotionally. Yet what we usually do is the opposite. We meet anger with more anger, often at equal or higher intensity, and unsurprisingly, the situation worsens.

The Bhagavad Gita names this trap precisely. Krishna describes krodha (anger) as one of the three gateways to self-destruction, alongside lobha (greed) and kama (excessive desire). And crucially, the Gita traces anger’s root not to the other person but to within: desire meets obstruction, and the frustration of that desire becomes rage. The enemy, in other words, is already inside before the argument even starts.

Insecurity: The Hidden Trigger Behind Our Reactions

I can understand when someone who doesn’t see anger as a form of uneasiness reacts with anger in return. But what really intrigued me was noticing how often I did the same thing, even after understanding what anger might represent. That led me to a second observation: we are bundles of insecurities.

The moment we are not at peace, we cling to our insecurities. Any hint of guilt, weakness, or discomfort can make us grab onto them as a shield. Then, when someone shows anger toward us, those insecurities get triggered. Instead of trying to understand the other person’s uneasiness, we react from our own. We respond with anger not because it helps, but because it feels like a way to protect ourselves.

So instead of calming the situation, both sides are now reacting from a place of inner disturbance: two uneasy people, both feeling threatened, both convinced they are right, and neither truly expressing what they really feel.

For those of us in leadership roles, this plays out in feedback conversations, performance discussions, and cross-team escalations more than anywhere else. When a team member pushes back on a decision with visible frustration, the easiest path is to match their energy or shut the conversation down with authority. Neither works. What usually works is naming the unease: “I notice this feels loaded. What’s the real concern here?” That one question has, more than once, changed the entire trajectory of a conversation.

Laziness, Discipline, and the Stories of Our Victimhood

More recently, I noticed a third thread. We are all, in some way, naturally lazy unless we consciously choose otherwise. Left to ourselves, we tend to choose what is easy, comfortable, and familiar. It often takes discipline and deliberate routine to pull us out of that default state.

Bring this back to anger. When we are not disciplined and someone asks us to do something that demands effort or change, we feel uneasy. That discomfort pokes at our laziness, and instead of admitting, “I don’t feel like doing this,” we may get irritated or angry. Once again, anger shows up not as a simple emotion but as a mask covering our unease and resistance.

And then comes the most fascinating layer: we are natural storytellers. When we feel lazy, insecure, or guilty, we often start narrating our way to innocence. We become the hero, or more often the victim, of an inner story. We convince ourselves that the world is unfair, that others are demanding too much, that we are being misunderstood or mistreated.

These stories make our anger feel justified. Instead of seeing our uneasiness, insecurities, or laziness, we focus on how unreasonable the other person supposedly is. The more we repeat these narratives, the more real they feel, and the harder it becomes to take an honest look at what is truly going on inside us.

The Vedantic idea of maya is useful here: not just illusion about the world outside, but the illusions we build about ourselves. The story of our own victimhood may be the most persistent maya of all.

The Observer: Watching Without Becoming

Between the trigger and the reaction, there is a gap. Most of the time, we don’t notice it. But the gap exists, and that gap is where choice lives.

The contemplative traditions call this the witness: the part of awareness that can observe what is arising without immediately becoming it. In practical terms, it means noticing the tightening in the chest before the words come out. It means catching the story as it forms: “They are doing this to me again.” It means pausing long enough to ask, “What am I actually feeling right now, and is that what I am about to express?”

This is not about suppression or spiritual bypassing. It is about developing a small but reliable capacity to see our own inner weather without immediately acting on it.

Choosing a Different Response

When I put all these observations together, anger starts to look very different. It becomes less of a problem to be crushed and more of a message to be decoded. Behind anger, there is often uneasiness. Behind our reaction to anger, there are usually insecurities. And underneath both, there may be a quiet laziness that resists the effort required to change, grow, or communicate honestly.

This doesn’t mean we should tolerate harmful behavior or stay silent when boundaries are crossed. But it does mean we can pause and ask different questions: What is this person struggling to express? What am I feeling insecure about right now? Is my resistance coming from genuine disagreement, or from discomfort with the effort involved?

Even a small shift in how we see anger, in ourselves and in others, can change the tone of a conversation. Instead of automatically escalating, we can experiment with listening, asking calm questions, or simply choosing not to add fuel.

A Quiet Invitation to Look Within

Anger will keep showing up in our lives; that much is certain. But each time it does, we have a choice. We can treat it as an enemy to attack or as a signal inviting us to look deeper.

If we see anger as uneasiness trying to find a voice, as insecurity trying to protect itself, and as laziness resisting effort, then every angry moment becomes a small opportunity. Not to fix other people, but to notice what is happening inside us: our discomfort, our defensiveness, our stories of victimhood.

From there, we can respond a little more gently, to others and to ourselves. And perhaps, over time, that gentle awareness can do what anger never manages to do: bring a bit more ease into our minds, our conversations, and our lives.
