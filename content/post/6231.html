Unlocking the Power of Experience: How Prompting in AI Can Elevate Your Knowledge!

A junior developer sees AI as a shortcut. I see it as a collaborator. Here is why experience changes everything about how you prompt.

In my first job in Bangalore in 2000, I was a software engineer trainee with a simple belief: if I could think logically, step by step, everything would fall into place.

Then I met my first real blocker.

My manager would not let me send emails.

Not “try harder” or “be careful”. Literally: no emails to our US counterparts. No code check-ins. No commits. And I was furious.

I remember thinking: This is exactly why people say managers are bad.

But the reality was more nuanced, and it taught me the most enduring lesson of my career.

The two-hour email

Our US counterparts sat in Tucson, Arizona. The overlap window was small.

If we asked the wrong question, or communicated poorly, the cost was not “some back-and-forth”. The cost was a full day.

So my manager would do something that felt ridiculous at the time.

They made me sit next to them for up to two hours, rewriting a single email.

First question:

“What do you want as a response?”

My answer was always some version of:

“I want them to either approve the design or tell me if I am fundamentally wrong.”

Then the editing began.

They would reorder sentences.

They would delete a line I felt was “important”.

They would highlight one sentence and say, “This is the only thing they need to respond to.”

By the time we hit send, my original email barely existed.

At first, I read it as power.

Later, I saw it as craft.

Because the responses we got back were exactly what we needed. Approval. Or a clear correction. In minutes. Inside that thin overlap window.

That email saved days.

And eventually, after about a year of this, my manager let me write and send emails on my own.

The surprising part is: I have been using that same mental model ever since.

A concrete AI example: two prompts, two different worlds

Fast-forward to today.

A junior developer on a team will often treat AI like a vending machine.

They type something like:

“Can you write a design for this feature?”

They get something clean, confident, and… generic.

It sounds correct because it is written well. But it does not engage with the real risks.

It does not ask the hard questions.

It does not reveal what it does not know.

Now here is the “experienced” version of the same task.

Instead of asking for “a design”, I ask the AI to play the role I need, with the constraints I actually have.

“You are a senior engineer reviewing a design proposal. Context: we have only a 30–60 minute overlap with the US team, so we need to minimize back-and-forth. Goal: produce a one-page design email that gets an approval or a specific correction in one reply.

Feature: [brief description].

Constraints: [existing system, latency, data model, whatever is real].

Please:

  1. Ask the 5 most important clarifying questions first.
  1. Then draft the email in a format that makes it easy to approve.
  1. Include a short “what could go wrong” section with 3–5 risks.”

The difference is not the model.

The difference is that the second prompt contains:

  • Context
  • A clear outcome
  • A defined role
  • Constraints
  • A response format
  • And most importantly: a sensitivity to the cost of a bad response

The real insight: prompting well requires knowing what “bad” looks like

A junior developer can be smart and hardworking and still struggle to prompt well.

Not because they lack vocabulary.

Because prompting is not just about asking.

It is about recognizing.

Recognizing when an answer is:

  • technically plausible but operationally impossible
  • correct in theory but risky in practice
  • missing the one question that will block the project later
  • confidently vague

That recognition takes time.

It comes from getting burned by ambiguity.

It comes from sending the wrong email and losing a day.

It comes from seeing a project drift because nobody forced clarity at the start.

In other words:

Prompting well requires knowing what a bad answer looks like.

And that knowledge takes years.

Why I no longer see AI as a shortcut

AI is powerful, but it is not magic.

If you treat it like a shortcut, you will mostly get shortcuts back.

If you treat it like a collaborator, you will start doing what good collaborators do:

  • you give context
  • you align on outcomes
  • you define constraints
  • you ask for the response format you need
  • you iterate with intention

That is not “prompt engineering”.

That is simply communication.

It is the same lesson my manager taught me, one two-hour email at a time.
