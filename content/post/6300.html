Can we compare reading books simmilar to Pratyahara (One of the eight limbs of Yoga)?

I meet people every day who confess, almost apologetically, that they are not readers.

Some say they fall asleep after a page. Others say they simply get bored. And honestly? I get it. I’ve been there too. The thickness of a book has a way of deciding your motivation level before you’ve even opened the cover. It sits on the nightstand like a silent accusation.

Here’s what I’ve noticed though. We don’t have an attention problem. We have a design problem.

Think about why we love reels. They’re over in 15 seconds. Web series are engineered in bite-sized episodes. Even a four-hour film like Dhurandhar holds people with lower attention spans in their seats because the filmmaker does the heavy lifting. The sound effects, the visual hooks, the pacing all of it is handed to you. You just have to show up.

A book is different. Brutally, beautifully different.

It gives you letters. That’s it. The visuals, the soundtrack, the emotional atmosphere you have to build all of that inside your own head. The author can write a gripping scene, but you have to conjure the fog, the footsteps, the tension in the room. That is not a flaw in the medium. That is the entire point. A book doesn’t just ask for your time. It asks for your presence. And in doing so, it quietly does something to your nervous system that no reel, no podcast, no show can replicate.

The Science Behind the Stillness

Reading isn’t just entertainment. It’s a physiological intervention.

When you settle into a book and your attention narrows to a single thread of words, something measurable begins to happen in your body. Ancient brain circuits — the same ones that evolved for sustained focus — begin to activate. Your heart rate slows. Your breathing evens out. The muscles you didn’t even realise were clenched begin to release.

What’s happening beneath the surface is a fundamental shift in your autonomic nervous system. Most of us spend our days in sympathetic dominance — the “fight-or-flight” mode that keeps us alert, reactive, and exhausted. Reading, when done with genuine absorption, gradually walks you away from that edge and into the parasympathetic state: “rest-and-digest.” The body stops bracing. It begins to recover.

And fiction does something even more remarkable. Reading about a character running through a forest activates your motor cortex — the same region that fires when you run. Reading about grief activates your brain’s empathy circuits as if the loss were your own. Neuroscientists call this “embodied reading.” The story doesn’t just entertain you. It becomes a low-stakes rehearsal for real life, quietly building neural pathways for experiences you haven’t yet lived.

How to Make Reading Work for You

Knowing the science is one thing. Building the habit is another. A few principles that actually help:

Mix your reading. Nonfiction sharpens your analytical networks and feeds your professional mind. But fiction is where the deepest stress relief lives — it fully disengages you from problem-solving mode in a way that nonfiction rarely does. You need both.

Time it strategically. Reading before bed isn’t just a cozy ritual. It lowers circulating stress hormones and nudges the parasympathetic system into gear, preparing the brain for sleep in a way that scrolling actively prevents.

Build a ritual around it. A consistent chair, a consistent time, a cup of something warm — these cues train your brain to shift states faster. Over time, the ritual itself begins to relax you before you’ve read a single word.

Follow curiosity, not obligation. The nervous system benefits of reading depend entirely on genuine absorption. If you are reading a book out of duty and finding your eyes sliding across the words without registering them, put it down. Pick up something that actually pulls you forward. A book you’re curious about is medicine. A book you’re forcing yourself through is just another task.

What Patanjali Knew Long Before the Neuroscientists

There is a concept in Patanjali’s Yoga Sutras called Pratyahara — the withdrawal of the senses. It is the fifth of the eight limbs of yoga, sitting precisely at the bridge between the outer practices and the inner work. In pratyahara, the yogi learns to pull their attention inward, away from the constant stimulation of the external world, so that the mind can begin to settle into itself.

I am not claiming that reading a novel is the same as a yogi’s meditative practice. But I do believe they share a profound family resemblance.

When you are truly absorbed in a book, you have, in a very real sense, withdrawn your senses from the noise of the world. The notifications lose their grip. The internal chatter quietens. Your attention, usually scattered across a dozen inputs, gathers itself around a single thread of meaning. The body follows. It always does.

In a VUCA world — volatile, uncertain, complex, ambiguous — this capacity to withdraw, to recollect yourself, is not a luxury. It is a leadership skill. The calmest people in the room are rarely the ones who have the fewest problems. They are the ones who have built the inner infrastructure to not be driven by every stimulus that arrives.

Books build that infrastructure. One page at a time. One evening at a time.

You don’t have to be a “reader” to start. You just have to be curious enough to pick up one book that interests you, and honest enough to put it down if it doesn’t.

The nervous system will do the rest.
